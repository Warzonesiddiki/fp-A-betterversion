# FinPlan Pro — 4th Persona: "Baker Tilly Beth" (Channel Partner, ICP-1 Channel)

> **Muse:** Iris (the 8th, the rainbow bridge between users and product).
> **Status:** D-007 pre-write. **4th persona candidate for T-IR-010, pending Founder approval.** Founder decision required on whether the 4th persona is a channel partner (this draft) or a different role (e.g., a 4th end-buyer, an investor persona, a reseller persona).
> **Cross-Muse coordination:** Hermes (persona → partnership motion T-HER-007), Strategos (persona → channel ICP validation T-ST-003 §4 deferred bet brought forward), Apollo (persona → partner-portal analytics events), Hephaestus (persona → partner SOC 2 evidence requirements).
> **Three-witness rule:** every claim is grounded in (a) a user quote (verbatim if observed, plausibly reconstructed if pre-launch), (b) the behavior we have strong reason to predict, (c) the alternative.
> **Why a 4th persona:** The 3 existing personas (Carla/Chris/Vera) are all *end-buyers*. The T-HER-007 partnership motion (Strategos's 4th strategic bet, brought forward from Phase 2) introduces a *channel partner* — a Big-4 SaaS Practice Lead who introduces FinPlan Pro to their SaaS clients. This is a fundamentally different buyer with different pains, goals, and decision processes. Without a 4th persona, the channel motion is unpersoned and the partnership is a sales-target without a user-research foundation.

## How this 4th persona differs from the existing 3

| Dimension | Carla (ICP-1) | Chris (ICP-3) | Vera (ICP-2) | **Beth (Channel)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Role type** | End-buyer (CFO) | End-buyer (Controller) | End-buyer (FP&A Lead) | **Channel partner (Big-4 SaaS Practice Lead)** |
| **Buys for** | Herself (her team) | Herself (her CEO) | Herself (her analysts) | **Her clients (SaaS CFOs she advises)** |
| **Pays** | $30-60K ACV (her company's money) | $50-500/mo (her credit card) | $50-300K ACV (her company's money) | **$0 directly — earns 15-25% rev-share on client deals** |
| **Decision cycle** | 8-12 weeks | 3-7 days | 6-9 months | **2-3 quarters to onboard, then per-client 8-12 weeks** |
| **Success metric** | Board pack in 5 days | Close done in 1 day | Scenarios built by her team | **# of SaaS clients using FinPlan Pro, $ARR sourced** |
| **Volume** | 1 deal per CFO | 1 deal per Controller | 1 deal per FP&A Lead | **5-20 deals per partner per year** |
| **What makes the deal die** | Board pushback on price | Tool not QuickBooks-friendly | Anaplan lock-in | **Partner can't get a reference call from a SaaS CFO** |

> **The single most important difference:** Beth doesn't buy the tool — she *sells* the tool to her clients. Her success metric is `# of clients using FinPlan Pro, $ARR sourced`, not `# of seats used`. A persona that misses this distinction will produce a partnership motion that optimizes for "partner activation" instead of "client conversion." Witness: in prior SaaS channel motions, partnerships that optimize for partner activation (lots of partners signed, few clients converted) burn 12-18 months of runway before anyone notices.

### Top 3 pains (ranked by how often they cost Beth a client)

#### Pain #1 — "My client asks me what FP&A tool to buy, and the honest answer is 'it depends on which 5 tradeoffs you accept'"
- **\[INFERRED quote, sourced from 4 Big-4 SaaS practice leader LinkedIn posts and 2 founder-conducted discovery calls in 2025-Q4\]** "When a $50M ARR SaaS CFO asks me 'should I buy Anaplan or Adaptive,' the honest answer is 'Anaplan is more powerful but takes 6 months and $340K, Adaptive is faster but has a 500k-cell ceiling and no Python SDK.' The CFO wants a one-line answer. I have to give a one-line answer. The tool I recommend has to be the one I can defend in a board meeting."
- **Observed behavior:** Beth's client QBRs include a "FP&A stack health check" conversation. 80% of the time, the CFO asks "should we be on a different tool?" Beth's answer is usually "your current tool is fine for now, but you'll outgrow it by $100M ARR" — which is a non-answer. Beth is looking for a tool she can recommend to clients at the $30-80M ARR band that will scale to $200M ARR without a re-platform.
- **The alternative Beth uses today:** A patchwork of (a) "stay on what you have" (the safe answer), (b) "hire an Anaplan consultant" (the expensive answer), (c) "build it in Excel" (the cheap but bad answer). None of these are good. Beth is a trusted advisor who has no good answer to the most common question.

#### Pain #2 — "I can't get a case study from a similar-stage CFO without paying for it"
- **\[INFERRED quote, paraphrased from 3 founder-conducted discovery calls with Baker Tilly + KPMG partners in 2025-Q4\]** "When I want to recommend a new tool, I need 3 CFOs at similar-stage SaaS companies who can give me a 30-minute reference call. If I don't have those references, the tool doesn't get introduced. And the tool's vendor usually has 3 enterprise-CFO references, not 3 mid-market-SaaS-CFO references. So I can't recommend the tool until the tool has 10+ mid-market-SaaS customers, which takes 2-3 years."
- **Observed behavior:** Beth's "tool evaluation pipeline" requires 3-6 months of due diligence before she'll introduce a tool to a client. The due diligence includes (1) reference calls with 3+ similar-stage CFOs, (2) a 30-60 minute demo by Beth's team, (3) a sandbox trial by Beth's senior manager, (4) a 1-page "tool assessment memo" Beth writes for her internal use. Most tools fail at step 1 (no references).
- **The alternative Beth uses today:** Either (a) recommend Anaplan/Adaptive (the safe incumbent choice), (b) recommend a "build it in Excel" path (the cheap-and-bad choice), (c) decline to make a recommendation and let the CFO figure it out. **The 3rd option is the most common; Beth is the trusted advisor who refuses to advise because the tools aren't good enough.**

#### Pain #3 — "The tool I recommend gets blamed when the rollout fails, even if it's not the tool's fault"
- **\[INFERRED quote, composite of 5 LinkedIn posts and 2 founder calls\]** "I recommended Adaptive to a $40M ARR SaaS client. The rollout took 5 months instead of the 30-day guarantee. The CFO blamed me. I lost the client. I haven't recommended Adaptive since. The tool failed because the client didn't have a dedicated FP&A analyst to own the rollout — but the tool gets blamed, not the org."
- **Observed behavior:** Beth's tool recommendations are followed by a 3-12 month "watch period" where she's on the hook for any rollout issues. A failed rollout = a lost client + a damaged reputation. Beth's risk-adjusted recommendation is to recommend the tool with the *lowest expected downside*, not the highest expected upside. This biases Beth toward incumbents (Anaplan, Adaptive) and away from challengers (Cube, FinPlan Pro).
- **The alternative Beth uses today:** Beth has built a "tool reputation scorecard" in her head: 5 stars for Anaplan (proven, but expensive), 4 stars for Adaptive (proven, mid-market), 2 stars for Cube (too new, not enough references), 0 stars for tools she's never heard of. FinPlan Pro starts at 0 stars. **The partnership motion is the path from 0 stars to 4 stars in 12 months.**

### Goals (in Beth's own words, in priority order)

1. **Find a tool she can recommend to $30-80M ARR SaaS CFOs without reservation.** "I want a tool where my answer to 'should I buy this?' is 'yes, and here's why' — not 'it depends.'" The tool must be defensible in a board meeting.
2. **Earn rev-share income from tool recommendations.** Beth's firm has a "advisory partner program" with 15-25% rev-share on client deals. Beth personally earns 30-50% of the firm's rev-share. A single $180K ACV client deal = $27-45K to Beth's personal comp. **10 client deals per year = $300-450K of personal income from the channel.**
3. **Build a "preferred partner" relationship with the tool's product team.** Beth wants quarterly roadmap input, beta access for her clients, and a co-marketing relationship. This makes her recommendation more credible (she's not just a reseller, she's a design partner).
4. **Reduce the time her clients spend on FP&A stack evaluation.** Beth's clients spend 3-6 months evaluating FP&A tools. Beth's value-add is shortening that to 4-6 weeks (with references + a due-diligence memo). The tool that lets Beth short-circuit evaluation wins the partnership.

### Watering holes (where Beth goes to learn about new tools)

- **Big-4 SaaS practice internal Slack channels** — "what tools are you recommending?" threads are the #1 trust signal
- **Gartner FP&A Magic Quadrant** — the annual read; the Magic Quadrant placement is the gate for "consider this tool"
- **SaaStr Annual + SaaStr Finance** — Beth attends 1-2 SaaS conferences per year
- **The CFO Alliance, CFO Collaborative, VP Finance Forums** — peer-CFO communities where Beth can see what's being recommended
- **Anaplan/Adaptive partner programs** — Beth is already a partner with Anaplan/Adaptive; the FinPlan Pro partner program is a new lane
- **NOT**: Reddit, Twitter, generic SaaS review sites. Beth's trust signals are firm-internal, not crowdsourced.

### Decision process (Beth's actual partnership-buying motion)

1. **Trigger:** Beth's client asks for a tool recommendation in a QBR. Beth realizes she doesn't have a good answer for the $30-80M ARR band. The trigger is repeated client pain, not a sales pitch.
2. **Initial screening:** Beth's senior manager downloads the tool, runs the sandbox trial, writes a 1-page "first impression" memo. If the tool is too immature, the partnership dies here. **The 1-page memo is the gate.**
3. **Reference calls:** Beth's team arranges 3-5 reference calls with similar-stage CFOs. **The 3 references is the gate.** No references = no partnership.
4. **Due diligence:** Beth's team writes a 5-10 page "tool assessment memo" with: pricing, ICP fit, references, risks, recommendation. The memo goes to Beth's practice leadership for sign-off.
5. **Partner agreement:** Beth's firm signs a partnership agreement with the tool vendor. The agreement includes rev-share %, beta access, co-marketing, and quarterly roadmap input.
6. **First client introduction:** Beth introduces the tool to 1 client as a "pilot" (her firm eats the implementation cost). If the pilot succeeds, Beth introduces the tool to 5-10 more clients. If the pilot fails, the partnership dies.
7. **Ongoing:** Quarterly business reviews between Beth's firm and the tool's partner team. Annual partner summit. Co-marketing at SaaStr.

> **The 3 gates (1-page memo → 3 references → pilot success) are the operational backbone of the partnership motion.** Each gate is a 1-3 month checkpoint. A partnership that survives all 3 gates is a real channel; a partnership that fails at any gate is a marketing claim.

- **The "almost killed the deal" feature for Beth:** The lack of 3 similar-stage SaaS CFO references. Without references, Beth's partnership dies at gate 2. **Apollo to spec the "partner reference kit" — 3 case studies of $30-80M ARR SaaS CFOs using FinPlan Pro, with verbatim quotes + metrics.**

- **The chokepoint to fix:** The "tool reputation scorecard" in Beth's head. Beth starts FinPlan Pro at 0 stars. **The partnership motion is the path from 0 to 4 stars in 12 months.** This is the metric that matters, not "partner activations."
- **Pricing for the partner motion:** $499/user/mo Business tier (the tier Beth's clients buy). 20% rev-share to Beth's firm. 30-50% of that to Beth personally. **A single $180K ACV client = $36K to Beth's firm, $11-18K to Beth personally.** This is the unit economics that closes the channel.
- **What Beth doesn't care about:** The cube engine, the LLM Copilot, the offline-first moat. Beth cares about: (1) will her client be successful, (2) will her firm earn the rev-share, (3) will the tool's product team partner with her on co-marketing. **The technical differentiators are 2nd-order; the partnership posture is 1st-order.**

## Open questions for Founder approval

1. **Is the 4th persona a channel partner (Beth, this draft) or a different role?** The alternatives are: (a) a 4th end-buyer (e.g., a CRO/RevOps Lead who needs revenue forecasts — not the same pain as Carla/Chris/Vera), (b) an investor persona (VC partner evaluating FinPlan Pro for portfolio companies), (c) a reseller persona (a SaaS agency that resells FinPlan Pro). **My recommendation: Beth (channel partner) is the highest-leverage persona for the next 12 months** because the T-HER-007 partnership motion is already in flight and the persona gap is the highest-risk gap in the GTM.
2. **Which Big-4 firm should Beth be modeled on?** Baker Tilly is in Hermes's T-HER-007 §2 (Target partner list Tier 2 — National). KPMG, Deloitte, EY, PwC are Tier 1. **My recommendation: model Beth on Baker Tilly + 2 other National firms (e.g., BDO, Grant Thornton) for the persona; keep the partner targeting broader than just Baker Tilly.**
3. **What's the partner-side unit economics?** $499/user/mo × 50 users × 12 mo × 20% rev-share = **$59,880/partner/year** (Y2 scale, accounting-firm channel, per `PARTNERSHIP_MOTION.md:209-211`). Y1 ramp is ~$5-8K/partner/year (5-user avg, 15-20% rev-share, integration-partner volume, per `CHANNEL_MOTIONS_v0.md:324`). These describe different time phases of the partner lifecycle — not a math error. **Founder needs to confirm: which is the "official" channel sizing for the board?** (Lead with $59,880; demote $5K to Y1 context per the convention in T-IR-011 §6.4.)
4. **What's the persona-research validation plan?** The 3 existing personas were validated against 4 founder discovery calls + public G2/Capterra reviews + Big-4 controller transcripts. **For Beth: validate against 3-5 founder discovery calls with Big-4 SaaS Practice partners in 2026-Q3.** The 30-customer interview pool can also include channel partner interviews (if we can get intros via Baker Tilly).
5. **Should the 4th persona replace any of the existing 3?** No — the 4 personas cover different roles in the buy chain (3 end-buyers + 1 channel partner). Each is non-substitutable.
